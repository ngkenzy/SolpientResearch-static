## 1. Investment thesis

**What it does:** Intuit sells consumer tax software (TurboTax, Credit Karma) and small-business and accounting software (QuickBooks, Mailchimp). *Reported*

**How it makes money:** Mostly subscriptions and per-filing fees for TurboTax, plus recurring subscriptions for QuickBooks. The Global Business Solutions segment (QuickBooks and related) grew 16% in FY2026. *Reported (segment growth via a secondary summary; verify in the 10-K)*

**Why customers choose it:** Tax filing is high-stakes, tax rules change every year, and TurboTax has years of accumulated data and brand trust. QuickBooks is embedded in small-business bookkeeping and payroll workflows, which creates switching costs. *General knowledge; not checked against current filings*

**Competitive advantages:** Brand, distribution, tax-rule expertise, and data. Switching costs are high for QuickBooks users and moderate for TurboTax users. *Assumption*

**Growth drivers:** Small-business software adoption, payroll and payments, and AI-assisted products. *Reported in management commentary; verify*

**Major threats:** General-purpose AI tools that can prepare taxes or bookkeeping with little product-specific data. The company itself cut about 17% of its workforce (roughly 3,000 roles) in May 2026, with $300–340M in restructuring charges, and lowered its TurboTax revenue forecast. Shares fell about 14% that day. *Reported (Reuters/Yahoo report; verify exact figures in the 8-K)*

**AI opportunity:** Intuit's partnerships with OpenAI and Anthropic could put its products inside AI assistants, which would be an opportunity if usage converts to paid products. *Reported in press coverage; outcome unknown*

**What must be true:**
1. The revenue base keeps compounding high single digits for several years (FY2027 guidance is about 9–10%).
2. QuickBooks keeps its small-business customers as AI agents take over bookkeeping tasks.
3. TurboTax keeps pricing power even as AI tax tools improve.
4. Stock-based compensation (about 9.6% of revenue) does not keep rising faster than revenue.

**Bull thesis:** Revenue has grown 14% in FY2026 with operating income up 20%, the stock trades at about 12x forward non-GAAP earnings, and the market is pricing in AI disruption that has not yet shown up in the numbers.

**Bear thesis:** The consumer tax franchise is a commodity-like product that general AI can replicate. FY2027 growth is guided to slow to about 9–10%. The company's own restructuring signals that management sees pressure. Its GAAP P/E is about 20x, which is not cheap once stock compensation is counted.

## 5. Competitive position

**Major competitors:** H&R Block and Block (tax); Xero and Sage, plus Gusto and ADP for small-business bookkeeping; and general-purpose AI tools that can prepare taxes directly.

**Where Intuit is stronger:** Brand, scale, tax-rule expertise, and the QuickBooks ecosystem. Revenue growth (14%) is well above the tax-software peers in this comparison.

**Where Intuit is weaker:** Operating margin (18–27%) is below ADBE (35%) and ADSK (29%). ROE is below ADBE. TurboTax is the most exposed franchise to AI substitution.

**Balance sheet:** Stronger than most of the group in net-debt terms; similar to ADBE, stronger than CRM (which has $42B total debt).

**Valuation:** Cheapest of the group on forward P/E (12.1x), above ADBE (8.9x) and below CRM (15.0x) and ADSK (16.9x). The market is pricing Intuit as an AI loser more than the others.

**Expected return:** See section 8. Intuit's base-case return is similar to the peers' on a rough basis, with more downside in the bear case.

## 10. Buffett / Lynch lens

**Buffett-style analysis:**
- **Understandability:** High. Tax software and bookkeeping are easy to explain.
- **Durable moat:** Partly. QuickBooks has a real moat; TurboTax's is weakening.
- **Pricing power:** Good in QuickBooks, uncertain in TurboTax.
- **ROIC:** Not computed; ROE is about 24%.
- **Free cash generation:** Strong. SBC-adjusted FCF margin is about 31%.
- **Balance sheet:** Strong; net debt is small against cash generation.
- **Management and capital allocation:** Mixed. Share count is falling, but the recent repurchases were made at much higher prices, and the restructuring signals a rough year.
- **Ability to reinvest:** High, but AI capex and restructuring costs reduce the return on reinvestment for now.
- **Purchase price vs. intrinsic value:** Below the base value ($537) and the 35% margin price ($349). But the bear value ($289) is near the current price.

**Answer:** Intuit meets Buffett-style business-quality principles for QuickBooks and generally for the company. It only partly meets the moat requirement, because the TurboTax franchise faces a credible substitute. On valuation, it is inexpensive if the base case holds, but the margin of safety depends on whether the business can keep compounding. **Partial pass.**

**Lynch-style analysis:**
- **Classification:** Stalwart (EPS growing about 17% a year over five years, with growth slowing to about 9–10%). Lynch would call it a Stalwart, not a Fast Grower, given the guided slowdown. *Assessment*
- **Earnings growth:** Strong historically; slowing.
- **PEG:** About 1.2 on GAAP trailing earnings; about 1.3 using non-GAAP forward. PEG is only partly meaningful because growth is no longer stable.
- **Growth runway:** Moderate; depends on AI monetization.
- **Balance sheet:** Strong.
- **Simplicity of story:** Good on the surface, but harder now that AI could change both products.
- **Valuation relative to growth:** Reasonable on a forward basis; not cheap on GAAP.

### Capital allocation test

**Why should I own Intuit instead of a broad-market index?**

The answer depends on whether you believe the business can keep growing high single digits for several more years. If it does, the stock returns roughly 7–9% a year from today's price, which is about the same as a reasonable index assumption, with a dividend. Owning Intuit only beats the index clearly if the bull case plays out, which requires growth to stay near double digits.

**The evidence does not yet support a compelling case that Intuit beats the index with high confidence.** The current price is about 7% below the 35% margin-of-safety price on the base case, which is a real cushion. But the base-case growth path is my assumption, and the bear case shows an annualized loss of about 11% a year over three years.

**Classification: Attractive below $349.** The current price ($325) is below the 35% margin-of-safety price, so it meets that bar now. I would not describe it as "attractive at current price" without that qualifier, because the uncertainty about AI is large enough that the valuation could be wrong for reasons the model cannot capture.

*Comparison tickers (ADBE, CRM, ADSK) are summarized in section 5 and on the screen: ADBE is cheaper on forward P/E with a stronger ROE; CRM and ADSK are more expensive with weaker FCF-to-price. Intuit is the cheapest of the four on forward earnings but carries the most AI-substitution risk in its consumer franchise.*

## Final conclusion

**Is this a great business?** It is a very good one: revenue has grown from $9.6B to $21.4B in five years, with high free-cash-flow margins and a durable small-business franchise in QuickBooks. It is not clearly great, because TurboTax faces a credible AI substitute and the company's own restructuring signals management sees a real threat.

**Is the valuation attractive?** On the base case, yes. The stock trades at about 13x SBC-adjusted free cash flow and 12x forward non-GAAP earnings, compared with a 3-year average GAAP P/E near 60x. But that comparison is misleading on its own, because growth has slowed. A more defensible base-case value is $400–450, which still leaves a margin of safety.

**What return appears realistically achievable?** About 7–9% a year over 5–10 years in the base case, including a 1.7% dividend. The bull case is about 13–21% a year. The bear case is roughly flat to negative.

**What could permanently impair the investment?** A sustained loss of TurboTax pricing power to general AI tools, combined with QuickBooks customers moving to AI-native bookkeeping. Either would turn the 9–10% growth guidance into high single digits or lower, which would compress the multiple and the base case together.

**Does it justify allocating capital instead of owning the market?** Only in a limited way. The base case is roughly index-like, and the downside is worse than an index. Intuit justifies a position only for an investor who has a view that AI strengthens QuickBooks more than it hurts TurboTax, and who can hold through the drawdown the bear case implies. For most investors, a broad index is the better default. This is a research memo, not personalized advice.
